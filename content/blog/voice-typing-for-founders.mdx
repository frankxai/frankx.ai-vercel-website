This page is for founders who spend the best hours of the day writing messages. The proposition is small: dictate the first draft of your recurring writing, edit it on the keyboard, and keep voice only where your own timings show a gain. Investor updates, async decisions, customer follow-ups and hiring notes are the four places to start. Keep exact numbers, code identifiers and confidential conversations off the microphone. The [Wispr Flow guide](/guides/wispr-flow) covers setup and the full tool; this post gives you a seven-day protocol to test it against your own week.

I use Wispr Flow every day, mostly to speak long briefs into Claude Code agents. Spoken briefs come out long and layered: the constraints, the history, the reason behind the request. That is context I would never bother typing. Founder messages have the same problem. The typed version is short because typing is tiring, and the person on the other end loses the reasoning.

## Why does a founder's inbox suit dictation?

Most founder writing is explanation. You already know the answer. The cost is turning a decision you made in your head into sentences someone else can act on.

That is exactly where speaking is cheap. You talk through the decision the way you would to a cofounder, then you edit. The editing is still keyboard work, and that part does not get faster. The gain comes from skipping the blank-page stage.

Wispr markets the product with the line "Talk 4x faster." Treat that as their marketing claim. Your real number is time to a sendable message, which includes thinking, correcting and checking. The [speaking vs typing speed post](/blog/speaking-vs-typing-speed) walks through what the research measured and a five-minute test you can run on yourself.

## Which messages should a founder dictate first?

Pick messages that are long, recurring and forgiving of a light edit.

**Investor updates.** Dictate the narrative: what changed this month, what you learned, where you need help. Speak it as if you were on a call with your lead investor. Then paste the metrics table from your dashboard. Do not speak the numbers.

**Slack and async decisions.** A decision message needs five parts: what you decided, why, what it changes, who owns the next step, and when you will revisit it. Speaking those five parts takes under a minute and produces a message people can act on without a follow-up thread.

**Customer follow-ups.** Right after a call, while it is fresh, dictate the recap: what they asked, what you promised, the date. This is the message most founders skip because the call ran long and the next one is starting. Spoken, it fits in the gap.

**Hiring notes.** Interview scorecards and debrief notes get thin when typed late at night. Dictate them immediately after the interview, against your scorecard headings. Read them back before you submit; the judgment needs to be fair and specific, and you own every word.

**Inbox replies.** Short replies are faster to type. Dictate the ones that need a paragraph of reasoning: the partnership you are declining, the vendor you are pushing back on, the intro you are framing.

## What does a dictated decision message look like?

Here is the shape to speak, as plain sentences. You say it once; the app writes it into Slack.

```text
Decision: we are pausing the self-serve tier until January.
Why: support load from self-serve accounts is taking most of
our two engineers' week, and those accounts convert to paid
at a fraction of the rate of sales-led ones.
What changes: the signup page routes to a waitlist from Monday.
Owner: Priya updates the page; I tell the three active trials myself.
Revisit: first week of January, with the support numbers.
```

The structure is the useful part. If you speak it in this order every time, your team learns to read it in this order.

## The 7-day voice-first protocol

Run this in a normal week. Keep a note with two columns: message type and minutes to sendable.

1. **Day 1: set up the dictionary.** Add your company name, product names, cofounders, top ten customers, investors and any jargon the app gets wrong. Proper nouns are where dictation embarrasses you first.
2. **Day 2: replies only.** Dictate every inbox reply longer than three sentences. Type the short ones. Log the times.
3. **Day 3: Slack decisions.** Use the five-part decision shape above for every async decision you post. Read each one back before sending.
4. **Day 4: customer follow-ups.** Dictate a recap within ten minutes of every external call. Note whether you would have sent one at all without voice.
5. **Day 5: hiring notes.** Dictate interview notes against your scorecard headings, straight after each interview.
6. **Day 6: the investor update.** Dictate the narrative sections in one sitting. Paste the metrics. Edit on the keyboard. Time the whole update against last month's.
7. **Day 7: review the log.** Keep voice for the message types where your time to sendable dropped. Go back to typing for the rest. The [voice typing learning path](/learn/voice-typing) has the next exercises if you want to keep building the habit.

The review on day 7 is the point. A week of your own timings is better evidence than any vendor claim, including the ones on this page.

## Where does dictation fail?

Know these before you start, so a bad first day does not end the experiment.

**Precise numbers.** "Fifteen" and "fifty" sound close. "1.2 million" can come out several ways. Revenue, burn, runway, prices in a quote and dates in a contract should be typed or pasted from the source.

**Code identifiers.** Function names, environment variables, branch names and URLs come out mangled or "corrected" into English. Dictate the description of the change, then type or paste the identifiers. The [voice prompting post for AI architects](/blog/voice-prompting-for-ai-architects) covers how to speak technical briefs without fighting the transcription.

**Open offices and confidential topics.** Everyone near you hears the board conversation, the layoff plan or the candidate's salary. Background voices also lower accuracy. Dictate those in a closed room or type them.

**Thinking out loud before you know.** Dictation captures rambling with total fidelity. If you have not decided, you get a long message that also has not decided. Spend thirty seconds on the point before you press the key.

**Tone in sensitive messages.** Cleanup fixes grammar and filler words. Kindness in a hard message is still your job. Read anything about a person's job, pay or performance line by line before it goes out.
