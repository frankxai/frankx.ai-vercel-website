This page is for authors who want to draft a book by voice and keep the book sounding like them. The proposition: dictate the first draft of one chapter, revise it on the keyboard, and compare it against a typed chapter before changing your whole process. Keep drafting and editing as separate modes, load your character and place names into the dictionary first, and talk each scene from a short card. The [Wispr Flow guide](/guides/wispr-flow) covers the tool in full; this post is the author's workflow.

I dictate long briefs into AI agents every day with Wispr Flow, and the pattern I see there holds for prose. Spoken drafts come out long and layered, with detail I would never have bothered to type. For a brief, that detail is context. For a chapter, it is texture: the smell of the room, the thing a character almost says. You keep the good parts in revision and cut the rest.

## Why dictate a book draft at all?

For many writers the blank page is partly a speed problem. When you write slowly, you edit as you go, and every sentence gets judged before the next one exists. Many authors stall there.

Speaking moves faster than your inner critic. You tell the scene the way you would tell it to a friend across the table, then keep going. The draft that results is rougher than a typed one, and there is more of it.

Two caveats. Some authors think best with their fingers and will find dictation slower; one chapter of testing tells you which kind you are. And dictation speed is only one part of the cost. The [speaking vs typing speed post](/blog/speaking-vs-typing-speed) explains why time to finished text is the number that matters.

## How do you separate drafting voice from editing voice?

Treat them as two jobs done at different times, ideally on different days.

**Drafting voice** is loose and forward-moving. You speak in full sentences, you do not stop to fix a word, and you mark problems out loud with a flag word such as "bracket fix" that you can search for later. You let a scene run long.

**Editing voice** is slow and exact. You sit at the keyboard with the dictated draft, read every line, cut, reorder and rewrite. This is where your style gets applied.

Mixing them is the most common failure. If you start rewriting a dictated paragraph mid-session, you lose the momentum that made dictation worth doing. If you dictate during revision, you lose the precision revision needs. Voice commands for small fixes are fine in either mode. Wispr Flow's Command Mode can rewrite selected text on a spoken instruction, which is useful for "make this sentence shorter" during editing. Keep it for small, specific changes you then read.

Tune the tool for prose. Wispr Flow lets you set style and tone per app; for your manuscript app, choose the lightest cleanup available so it removes "um" and fixes punctuation without smoothing your rhythm.

## How should you set up the dictionary for a novel?

Do this before chapter one. Proper nouns are the first thing dictation gets wrong and the most tiring thing to fix.

Add:

- every character's first name, surname and nickname
- every place: cities, houses, ships, planets, streets
- invented words: magic systems, titles, slang, brand names inside the story
- recurring objects with unusual names
- real names you reference: authors, historical figures, foreign terms

Say each new name aloud once in a test document to check it lands. At the end of each drafting session, search the chapter for misspellings and add the misses to the dictionary. Wispr Flow's personal dictionary also learns names and jargon over time, so the list gets shorter.

## What is the talk-the-scene method?

Before each scene, write a card of four or five lines on paper or in a note. Then speak the scene from the card in one take.

The card holds:

1. **Who is in the scene and what each wants.**
2. **Where and when, with one concrete sensory detail.**
3. **What changes by the end.** If nothing changes, the scene probably goes.
4. **The last line or image, if you know it.**

Then talk. Describe the space as you would to someone with their eyes closed. Speak the dialogue in each character's voice. When you lose the thread, say "bracket" and what is missing, and carry on. Stop when you reach the change on the card.

The card is short on purpose. It gives you a destination without scripting the route, which keeps the spoken draft alive.

## The revision protocol

Dictated drafts need a specific revision routine. Run these passes in order, each on a separate sitting.

1. **Search the flags.** Find every "bracket" note and resolve it, or log it for later. Fix every proper noun the dictionary missed, then add it.
2. **Structure pass.** Read the chapter at speed for shape only. Does each scene reach the change on its card? Cut scenes that do not. Move paragraphs before you polish any sentence.
3. **Repetition pass.** Spoken prose repeats: the same word in nearby sentences, the same sentence opening, the restated thought. Search for your verbal habits ("just", "really", "and then") and cut most of them.
4. **Line pass by keyboard.** Rewrite sentence by sentence. This is where your style lives, and it is slow.
5. **AI suggestions, accept or reject.** If you use an AI assistant, ask it for specific notes such as inconsistencies, unclear pronouns or continuity errors, and apply each change yourself. Do not paste back a rewritten chapter. The [AI writing system guide](/guides/ai-writing-system) covers how to set up that review step.
6. **Read aloud.** Read the finished chapter aloud, start to end. Anything you stumble over gets fixed. You started with your voice; you finish with it.

Time the whole cycle for your test chapter and compare it with a typed chapter of similar length. Keep the method if the comparison and the prose both hold up. The [voice typing learning path](/learn/voice-typing) has practice drills for building the drafting habit.

## Where does dictation struggle for authors?

**Punctuation-heavy prose.** Dialogue tags, em-dashes and nested quotes come out inconsistent. Fix them in the line pass rather than speaking punctuation.

**Invented names you have not added.** Every new character or place needs a dictionary entry the day you invent it.

**Shared spaces.** Talking a love scene or a murder in a café is awkward, and background voices hurt accuracy. Draft somewhere private.

**Over-cleaned prose.** If the tool's style settings rewrite too much, your narrator starts sounding like a business email. Turn cleanup down for the manuscript app.
