Open a sketchbook to a clean page. Draw a crooked door in the bottom corner. Now there’s a question small enough to answer. Who would have to kneel to get through it?

In Arcanea’s *Bestiary of Creation*, the creature that haunts this moment is called the Blank Terror. It lives on empty pages, blank canvases and untouched instruments. Look at the empty space long enough and it seems to grow. The keeper’s advice is wonderfully physical. Put a mark on the surface.

[Read “Creatures of Beginning”](/books/arcanea-bestiary/chapter-01-creatures-of-beginning). The chapter presents the creatures as invented names for familiar experiences. Their habitats are half the pleasure of the book. A fantasy field guide gets to ask where an idea sleeps.

## Two creatures, two different afternoons

The Blank Terror can ruin an afternoon before the first line. The Perfect Phantom, another creature in the same chapter, can ruin one after you’ve written a perfectly good paragraph. It shows you the magnificent version you had in your head. Your paragraph has to compete with a book that has never had to find its second sentence.

You can give these creatures different jobs as a writer. Let the first one tell you when the scene is too large. A city at war is a lot to draw. A baker taking the bell off the shop door gives your hand somewhere to start. Let the second creature visit after you have two versions on paper. Then it can help you hear which sentence works.

## Draw the thing that keeps interrupting

Try a small entry of your own. Give the creature a habitat precise enough that somebody else could find it. Give it one behavior you could illustrate in a single frame. Give the keeper one ordinary object that changes the encounter.

Here’s a new example for the exercise. A moth lives in the gap between two unfinished verses. It lands on whichever line you read aloud twice. The keeper puts a saucer by the lamp and sings the second verse while the moth is busy. The moth belongs to this exercise. The Blank Terror and the Perfect Phantom belong to the book.

Leave one detail unexplained. Why a saucer? By the time you have an opinion, the page already has a story on it.
