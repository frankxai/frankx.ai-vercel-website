This page is for CEOs and executives whose work is mostly briefing other people. The proposition: dictate delegation memos, board narrative drafts and 1:1 notes, then edit on the keyboard. Spoken briefs carry the intent and constraints that typed ones drop. Before rolling voice out to a team, settle the plan, SSO, HIPAA and model-training questions below. I dictate long multi-role briefs into Claude Code agents every day with Wispr Flow. A recent one asked an agent to act as CEO, CMO and CTO for frankx.ai at once, audit every blog article, separate internal from public content, and work through a plan. Spoken, it came out long and layered, full of context I would never have bothered to type, and the agent needed that context. People you delegate to need the same context.

## Why do typed delegation memos fail?

A typed memo is short because typing is effortful at the end of a day of meetings. The thing that gets cut is the reasoning: why this matters now, what you already ruled out, what would make you unhappy with a technically correct result.

The person receiving the memo then guesses. Some guess well. Most come back with questions, or deliver against the literal words and miss the intent. Each round trip costs a day.

Speaking removes the effort barrier on the explanation. You still edit, and the edit still takes keyboard time. What changes is that the context gets onto the page in the first place.

## What goes into a dictated delegation memo?

Speak three sections every time. Over a few weeks your team learns to expect them.

**Intent.** What outcome you want and why it matters this quarter. One or two sentences on the business reason.

**Constraints.** Budget, deadline, people who must be consulted, things that are off-limits, approaches you already tried and rejected.

**Done criteria.** What you will look at to decide it is finished. Name the artifact, the date and the quality bar.

A spoken version, before editing, might read like this:

```text
Intent: I want our enterprise renewal rate back above where it was
last year before the board meeting, because the board will ask why it dropped.
Constraints: no new discounting without my sign-off. Talk to Sam in
finance before changing any contract terms. We already tried the
quarterly business review push in spring and it did not move the number.
Done: a one-page diagnosis of the five largest at-risk accounts,
an owner for each, and a plan I can approve by the 14th.
```

Read it back, cut the repetition, and send. The structure carries the quality.

## Can a CEO draft board materials by voice?

The narrative, yes. A board update has a story underneath the slides: what happened, what you learned, what you are changing, where you need the board's help. Dictate that story in one sitting as if you were telling your chair over coffee. Then structure and edit it on the keyboard, or with an AI writing assistant against your deck template.

Keep every number out of the dictation. Paste revenue, burn, pipeline and headcount from the source system. A misheard figure in board materials is expensive, and voice gives you nothing on numbers you already have in a spreadsheet.

## How should a CEO use voice for 1:1 notes?

Dictate notes straight after each 1:1, while you remember what was said and how it was said. Use the same three headings every time: what we discussed, what each of us committed to, what I am watching for.

Wispr's Notetaker runs on Mac and Windows without a meeting bot and works for in-person conversations; per Wispr, it offers MCP access so notes are usable in Claude or ChatGPT. Ask before recording anyone, follow your local consent rules, and keep anything about compensation or performance in your HR system rather than a general notes tool.

## The voice memo to brief to owner loop

This is the weekly protocol. It takes about thirty minutes on a Monday.

1. **Capture.** Through the week, dictate a quick note whenever you think "someone should own this." Do not structure it. Speak it into a single running doc.
2. **Brief.** On Monday, read the running doc. For each item still worth doing, dictate a delegation memo with intent, constraints and done criteria.
3. **Assign.** Name one owner per memo. Send it in the channel where that owner already works.
4. **Confirm.** Ask each owner to reply with their own one-sentence version of the done criteria. Mismatches show up here, before the work starts.
5. **Review.** The following Monday, check each memo against its done criteria before you add new ones. Close, re-brief or kill.
6. **Tune.** Add every name, product and acronym the dictation gets wrong to the dictionary. Shared dictionaries carry across the team on Pro and above.

## What privacy questions should a CEO ask before rollout?

Wispr states it is SOC 2 Type II and ISO 27001 certified and HIPAA-ready with a signed BAA, on its [trust centre](https://trust.wispr.ai). Those are the vendor's claims. Your security lead should verify the reports and ask the rest.

- **Where is audio processed and stored?** Wispr Flow processes dictation in the cloud. Ask about regions, retention periods and deletion.
- **Is model training off for everyone?** Any user can opt out on any plan. Growth gives admins control; Enterprise enforces it org-wide. Decide which level your policy needs.
- **Which apps should be blocked?** Enterprise can block dictation in chosen apps. List the systems where spoken content should never go through a third party.
- **Who can see shared dictionaries and snippets?** Shared vocabulary may contain customer names, code names and unannounced products.
- **What is the offboarding path?** With SCIM on Enterprise, access ends when HR deactivates the account. On lower tiers, someone has to remove people by hand.
- **Is cloud processing acceptable for every team?** If legal or R&D needs on-device processing, the [tool comparison](/blog/wispr-flow-vs-superwhisper-vs-macwhisper-vs-willow) covers options with local models.

Write the answers into a one-page policy and share it with the team before anyone installs the app. People dictate more freely when they know what is off-limits.

## Where does dictation fail for executives?

Numbers, as above. Confidential conversations in shared spaces. Names the dictionary has not learned yet. And the memo you start speaking before you have decided what you want, which comes out long and undecided. Thirty seconds of thought before pressing the key fixes most of that.
