# Chapter 2 — The Wolgadeutsche Line

## The Geography of Exile

To understand the hands that laid the bricks in Seesen, you have to understand the soil that shaped them.

My father was not born in Germany. He was born in Pavlovka, a rural settlement in northern Kazakhstan, on September 8, 1969. 

If you look at a map of Central Asia, Pavlovka sits in the vast, windswept steppe—a landscape of brutal extremes where summer temperatures push past forty degrees Celsius and winter blizzards sweep down from Siberia, burying fence lines under three meters of hard-packed snow.

German families did not arrive in Kazakhstan by choice. They arrived in cattle cars.

In August 1941, by decree of the Supreme Soviet, Joseph Stalin abolished the Autonomous Soviet Socialist Republic of the Volga Germans. In a matter of weeks, more than four hundred thousand people—descendants of farmers, craftsmen, and weavers who had answered Catherine the Great’s manifesto in the 1760s to cultivate the Volga river valley—were declared collective enemies of the state.

They were given twenty-four hours to pack what they could carry. They were loaded onto freight trains without heat, sanitation, or adequate food, and transported thousands of kilometers east into Siberia and the Kazakh steppe.

My great-grandfather, Christian Riemer, born in 1914, survived the *Trudarmee*—the forced labor army where Volga German men were separated from their families and worked under armed guard in mines, forests, and railway construction through sub-zero winters on starvation rations. Thousands died of typhus, dysentery, and hypothermia. 

Those who survived did so through an almost terrifying biological and psychological endurance. They did not have the luxury of therapy, emotional debriefs, or public recognition. Survival was simple arithmetic: keep breathing, keep your hands moving, share the black bread, and protect your children.

## The Identity of the In-Between

By the time my grandfather Alexander and his wife Paulina (born Schneider) were raising my father in the post-Stalin era, the Volga Germans occupied a strange, silent purgatory.

At home, behind closed doors and double-glazed winter windows, they spoke an archaic, eighteenth-century dialect of German—a language preserved like amber, untouched by the modern evolution of Germany itself. They prayed from old Lutheran hymnbooks and carried German family names: *Riemer, Gorte, Reisig, Schneider, Kraus*.

Outside the front door, they spoke Russian. In school, in the military, and on official identity documents, the fifth line of the Soviet passport (*Natsionalnost*) stamped them permanently: **НЕМЕЦ (German)**.

In the Soviet Union, being German meant carrying an invisible asterisk. You could be the best mechanic in the district, the most dependable combine operator, or the most skilled carpenter, but certain university doors were quietly closed, certain party promotions never arrived, and in moments of neighborhood tension, the old wartime slur would slip out: *Fashisty*.

Then came the fall of the Berlin Wall and the collapse of the Soviet empire. 

In the 1990s, the Federal Republic of Germany opened its doors to ethnic Germans through the *Spätaussiedler* law. Millions of families packed their lives into shipping containers and boarded westward trains and planes, believing they were finally coming *home*.

What they found was another border.

## Arriving Without a Handout

When my parents stepped onto German soil with two young children, they did not find a welcoming embrace. They found cold municipal bureaucracy, crowded transit camps (*Aussiedlerwohnheime*), and a society that looked at them sideways.

To modern West Germans in the nineties, these immigrants did not look like countrymen. They wore clothes purchased in Alma-Ata. They spoke German with a distinct Slavic cadence, or spoke Russian to each other in supermarket aisles. To the locals, they were not Germans returning from sixty years of forced exile; they were *die Russen*—economic migrants arriving to claim benefits.

My father’s Soviet technical diplomas and master craftsmanship certifications were deemed non-equivalent by the German chamber of trades. On paper, thirty years of experience building homes and running machinery evaporated into a blank sheet.

Many men in that situation broke. 

I watched men of my father's generation—capable engineers, agronomists, and foremen—succumb to chronic depression, heavy drinking, and bitter resentment. They spent their days raging at the bureaucrats at the *Arbeitsamt*, feeling humiliated that their life's work was treated as worthless by the very country that had claimed to repatriate them.

My father took one look at that trap and closed the door.

He never went to a demonstration. He never filed an appeal. He never complained about discrimination at the dinner table.

*“If they don't give you papers,”* he told me once while sharpening a wood chisel, *“you make your work so good that only a fool would ask for the paper.”*

He took the lowest-paying, hardest masonry and framing jobs on local German construction crews. While others took coffee breaks, he kept laying brick. While others grumbled about rain and mud, he finished the gable. Within two years, German contractors were calling him directly on the home landline: *“Witali, can you take the roof? Witali, we have an uneven foundation only you can true.”*

He did not demand respect. He made himself **undeniable**.

## The Lessons of the Unclaimed Line

From the Wolgadeutsche lineage, I inherited three foundational laws that govern every system I build today:

### 1. Sovereignty Is Not Granted; It Is Constructed
If you wait for an institution, an employer, an industry association, or a platform algorithm to give you official recognition, you are already a subordinate. True authority does not come from a badge or a blue checkmark. It comes from the unmistakable weight of what you produce.

### 2. The Refusal of Resentment
Resentment is the emotional weapon of the dependent. When you resent someone for not giving you what you deserve, you concede that they have the power to define your worth. The moment you decide that your output belongs to your own standards, resentment disappears and is replaced by clean, cold execution.

### 3. Build Your Own Ground
If the ground beneath your feet belongs to someone else—whether it is a landlord’s property, a corporation’s platform, or an empire’s tolerance—they can pull it out from under you with a stroke of a pen. The only real security is sovereign ownership: your own land, your own tools, your own code, your own distribution, your own line.

Yet, this extraordinary survival ethos carried a hidden poison.

Because when you believe that survival depends entirely on your own solitary grit, you develop a dangerous pathology: **the inability to stop.**

And that brings us to the tragedy of the lone builder.
