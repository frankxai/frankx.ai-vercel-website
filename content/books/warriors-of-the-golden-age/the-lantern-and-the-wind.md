# The Fable of the Lantern and the Wind

## A fable of the Foundation Gate

---

Between one Realm and the next, the corridor-road runs a long way through the dark, and the people who keep the lamps along it will tell you that dark has its own traffic. They hear it pass, some nights, close against the glass. Unlit and empty are two different words in a lamp-keeper's mouth.

There was an old woman who kept the last lamp on a lonely stretch, where the road bent around a shoulder of bare rock and a traveler coming late could lose the way in the space of ten steps. Her lamp was nothing grand: a wick, a reservoir of oil, a chimney of poor glass gone grey with smoke at the top, all of it inside a little stone housing she had built up with her own hands to break the wind. People in the settled places thought lamp-keeping was work for those too old to do anything braver. She never troubled to correct them. Every dusk she climbed to the lamp, trimmed the wick so it would burn clean and not choke on its own char, filled the oil, and lit it from a coal she kept banked in an iron box against her chest, so that even in the rain she always had one small fire to give the big one.

The night the wind came, it came from the wrong side.

It came around the rock instead of straight down the road, so the stone housing she had built against the road-wind did nothing at all. The flame lay down sideways. It guttered blue at the root. She put her own body into the gap and cupped her two hands around the little heat, and that was when the other thing began to speak, the way it likes to pick the worst nights for speaking.

*Let it go out,* it said. Not loud. Reasonable. Almost kind. *There is no one on the road. You have watched all night, and no one has come. You are old, and the wind is cold, and the oil is nearly gone. There is no one out there for the light to save. Let it out. Go down and sleep. Who in the world could blame you.*

She did not answer it. She had nothing to tell it that it did not already know. She trimmed the wick again where the wind had fouled it, pinched off the black curl, and the flame stood up a finger taller.

*You cannot keep it lit the whole night,* the voice said. *You know that you cannot. Why suffer the entire dark for a light that fails at the fourth hour anyway? Let it fail now, and spare yourself the middle. It is only good sense.*

That one was harder, because it was very nearly true. She could not know that she could keep it. She poured in the last of the oil a little at a time, so the flame would not drown in its own fuel, and she turned her back full into the wind, so that her back took the cold and her hands kept the warm. She stayed like that, bent over one small flame on a black road, doing the only work the night had left in it: the wick, the oil, the wick again.

The voice came once more, in the low hour before the turn of the night, and this time it did not argue. It only said, very softly, that she was alone out here. She let that be true. She was. She fed the coal from the iron box when the wick guttered and nearly went, and she caught it, and brought it back, and did not let it go.

The traveler came just before the grey of morning. A man half-frozen and well off his path, who for the last mile had walked toward the one thing he could see in all that country, which was a small steady light around the shoulder of a rock. He did not know there had ever been a question about it. He warmed his hands at the housing and thanked her for keeping such a good lamp, as though keeping it had been the easiest thing in the world, as though the light had simply been there all night the way the rock was there.

She let him think it. On the whole, it is better that they think it.
