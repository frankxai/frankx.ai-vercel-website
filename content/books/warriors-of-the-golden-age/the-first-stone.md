# The Fable of the First Stone

## A fable of the Foundation Gate

---

There is a Realm in the low country that was settled twice. The first people came, and built quickly, and did not last a generation. When the second people came, they walked in through what the first had left behind: walls lying down in the grass like tired animals, a well gone sour, a hall whose floor had heaved until the flagstones stood up on end like bad teeth. They were not fools, the first people. They had only been in a hurry, and the ground had noticed.

Among the second people was a mason named Berach Stonhal. He was not their leader. He was the one they sent for when a thing had to stand up and go on standing.

They set him to build the granary before the houses, because a people who cannot keep their grain dry do not get to argue about much else. So Berach dug. He dug down past the black soil and the yellow soil and into the grey, where the spade rang instead of bit, and there he stopped, because grey ringing ground is ground that will hold. Then he ran his lines. He stretched cord between the batter-boards and squared his corners with a knotted rope, three lengths and four lengths and five, the old trick that gives a true right angle to a man who owns no better tool. He hung his plumb-bob and watched it until the weight swung dead still and told him where down honestly was.

Then he began the footing course. It is the lowest course and the widest, and it carries every stone that will ever be set above it. He chose each stone for its bed, turning it in his two hands until it lay the way it had lain in the earth, because a stone set against its grain will crack in ten winters and a stone set with its grain will hold for a thousand. He packed the hearting tight between the faces, the small stones and the chips, so the core could not shift. Every few feet he set a through-stone, one long stone reaching from face to face, tying the wall to itself so it could never come apart down the middle.

A wall a man could step over took him past midsummer.

A boy carried hearting stones for him that summer, in a leather apron, until his shoulders ached, and sorted the chips by size the way Berach showed him. The boy asked once who the granary was for. "For us," Berach said. "For the winter." He set a stone, and rocked it, and felt it take his weight. Then he thought better of the answer. "And for whoever is standing here in a hundred winters, who will never know our names. Mostly them. There are more of them than there are of us."

The reeve came to watch, and then came to complain, which is one of the things a reeve is for. "The rains are three weeks off," he said. "You are laying stone we are only going to bury. Not one soul will ever set eyes on this course again, once the dirt goes back over it. Lay it quick and get to the next one, the one we can actually see."

Berach did not stop working while the man talked. He fit a stone, and rocked it, and let it settle. "You are right that no one will see it," he said. "That is the reason it has to be true. The courses people can see, those get looked at, and a fault that gets looked at gets mended. This one only ever gets stood on. Nobody climbs back down here to fix it. So it had best be right the once."

"Stood on by whom? It is going under the ground."

"By all of it," Berach said, and he tipped his head up at the wall that was not there yet, the whole granary hanging in the air above his hands where only he could see it. "Every course I lay above this one lays its weight straight down onto it. The grain leans on the wall. The wall leans on this. I will not be alive to prop it up the year it gets tired. So I would rather not build it tired."

The reeve looked across the field at the old ruin, at the heaved floor and the flagstones standing on end. He said nothing more about the rains. He was a hurrying man in a hurrying settlement, and it cost him real time to stand in that trench and let a wall go up slowly, and he paid it, and stayed, and handed stones.

The dirt went back over the footing course before the week was out, and no one ever saw it again. Berach laid the courses above it, the ones with his name half-remembered on them. Later he laid courses in other walls, and later still he grew old, the way even careful men do.

The granary is standing yet. It stood through the wet year that stripped the roofs off three of the newer houses, and the grain inside it kept dry, and a great many children have slept the whole winter through on the sacks in there without once thinking about the ground under the floor. That is what the ground under a floor is for. Ask the people who live there now who laid the first course, and they will look at you the way you would look at a person who asked who is holding up the field.
