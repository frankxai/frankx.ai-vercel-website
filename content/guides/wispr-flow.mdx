## What does Wispr Flow actually do?

You hold a hotkey, speak, and release. Wispr Flow transcribes the audio in the cloud, removes filler words, adds punctuation, applies a style for the app you are in, and pastes the result at your cursor. It works system-wide rather than inside one editor.

The parts that matter in daily use:

- **Personal dictionary.** It learns names and jargon, and you can add terms yourself. Accuracy on your own vocabulary depends more on this list than on anything else.
- **Snippets.** A spoken trigger expands into saved text, such as a scheduling link or a standard sign-off.
- **Per-app styles.** Casual for chat, more formal for email, as you configure it.
- **Command Mode.** Select text, speak an instruction ("make this three bullet points", "shorter"), and Wispr rewrites the selection. It works on the text you select.
- **Languages.** More than 100, per the vendor.
- **Notetaker.** Meeting and voice-memo notes on Mac and Windows, without a bot joining the call. Wispr's pricing page says the notes can be used from Claude and ChatGPT through MCP.

Wispr's own marketing says you will "talk 4x faster". Treat that as the vendor's claim. The research on speaking versus typing is narrower than the headlines, and the [speed article](/blog/speaking-vs-typing-speed) covers what it does and does not show.

## How do you set up Wispr Flow in ten minutes?

1. **Install and sign in.** [Download Wispr Flow](/go/wispr) for Mac or Windows, and the mobile app if you write on your phone. Grant microphone and accessibility permissions when asked; without accessibility access it cannot paste into other apps.
2. **Choose the hotkey.** The default push-to-talk key works. Pick one you can hold without looking. Hands-free mode is there for long dictation.
3. **Pick the microphone.** A headset or a decent USB mic beats a laptop mic in a noisy room. Set it explicitly instead of trusting the system default.
4. **Fill the dictionary.** Add your name, your company, your product names, your team, and the tools you mention daily: Claude Code, Cursor, Supabase, Vercel, whatever your stack is. Ten minutes here saves corrections every day.
5. **Set app styles.** Decide how formal email should be and how casual chat should be.
6. **Create three snippets.** For example, your calendar link, your standard intro and your sign-off.
7. **Check privacy settings.** Decide whether to opt out of model training and check what context the app may read, before you dictate anything confidential.

## How do I use it?

Most of my dictation goes into Claude Code. A typical brief is spoken in one take: who the agent should act as, what the goal is, what already exists, what to leave alone, and how I will know it is done. Typed, a brief like that would be three lines. Spoken, it runs to several paragraphs of context the agent actually needs.

That is the pattern I would teach first. Voice is cheap for context and expensive for precision. A spoken brief is long and layered because talking costs almost nothing; a typed brief is short because typing does. Agents do better with the long version.

What I still type: file paths, function names, exact numbers, regular expressions, anything secret, and edits to a single word. Those are faster and safer on the keyboard.

## What should a spoken brief to a coding agent contain?

Use the same six parts every time, in this order. Saying them out loud in a fixed order keeps a ramble from turning into a mess.

```text
Role: act as [the senior role you need] for [project].
Goal: the outcome is [one sentence a reviewer could check].
Context: what exists today is [...]. The reason we are doing this is [...].
Constraints: do not touch [...]. Keep [...] the same. Ask before [...].
Done when: [tests pass, the page renders at 375 px, and the PR is open as a draft].
Verify by: run [command] and show me [evidence] before you call it done.
```

Speak it, read the transcript, fix any misheard identifiers by typing them, then send. If the transcript wanders, select it and use Command Mode with "restructure this into role, goal, context, constraints, done criteria". The [AI architect article](/blog/voice-prompting-for-ai-architects) goes further, with dictionary entries for repository names and a review checklist.

## Which work should you speak, and which should you type?

| Speak it | Type it |
| --- | --- |
| Agent briefs and prompts | File paths, identifiers, commands |
| Email and Slack replies | Exact figures, prices, dates in contracts |
| Meeting follow-ups and delegation notes | Passwords, keys, client secrets |
| First drafts of posts, scripts and chapters | One-word edits and final polish |
| Journaling and idea capture | Anything you would not say aloud in the room you are in |

The rule underneath the table: speak where context matters more than exact characters, and type where a single wrong character costs you.

## Is Wispr Flow safe for confidential work?

Know three facts before you dictate anything sensitive.

1. **Processing happens in the cloud.** Audio leaves your machine. There is no offline mode.
2. **Model training is opt-out.** Wispr says every plan can opt out at any time, Growth lets admins set it for the team, and Enterprise locks it off.
3. **Certifications are vendor-stated.** Wispr lists SOC 2 Type II and ISO 27001, and HIPAA-ready dictation with a signed BAA. Your security team should read the documents on the [Trust Center](https://trust.wispr.ai/) rather than rely on a pricing page or this guide.

Context features in dictation apps can read what is on screen in the active window. Read Wispr's current privacy policy and settings to see what is collected on your plan.

For client work under NDA: opt out of training, keep secrets and identifiers on the keyboard, and do not dictate in shared spaces. If your policy says audio may not leave the device, Wispr Flow is the wrong tool; test an on-device option instead.

## How do you know if voice typing is working for you?

Run this for seven days.

1. **Pick one message type** you send at least daily: Slack replies, agent briefs, follow-up emails.
2. **Day one, typed baseline.** Time three messages from opening the app to pressing send.
3. **Days two to seven, spoken.** Dictate the same message type. Time it the same way, including the read-back and corrections.
4. **Log errors.** Note every misheard word that would have embarrassed you if you had sent it unread.
5. **Decide.** Keep voice for that message type if it is faster to a sendable draft and the error log is shrinking. Add the next message type the following week.

The number that matters is time to a sendable message. Words per minute leave out review and correction, and speaking fast then correcting for two minutes loses to typing.

## What fails, and how do you fix it?

- **Names come out wrong.** Add them to the dictionary. Proper nouns are the most common failure and the easiest to fix.
- **Long dictation wanders.** Use the six-part brief order, or dictate in two or three short takes.
- **Open office or café.** Use a headset, or keep voice for when you are alone. Whispering hurts accuracy.
- **Code identifiers get mangled.** Type them. Voice is for the sentence around the identifier.
- **You stop reading before sending.** Read every transcript. A confident wrong word reads worse than a typo.
